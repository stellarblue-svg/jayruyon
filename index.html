About

For nearly 20 years, Jay Runyon has translated dense clinical research, public health data, and quality-improvement science into writing that primary care physicians, care teams, and patients can actually put to use. Along the way she's carried titles beyond "writer" — project facilitator, program manager, practice transformation lead — and that operational background shows up in the writing itself: an instinct for what a clinician needs to know to act, not just to read.

"Writing has always been the throughline — whatever the title on the door said."

Her work spans patient and clinician education, immunization and public health campaigns, mental health care communication, practice transformation, and health IT and telemedicine policy, developed for the New Jersey and Delaware Academies of Family Physicians, state health departments, and independent healthcare clients. She now writes independently as Stellar Blue Communications.

The Telemedicine Revolution Full text
Runyon J, Barrett TJ. Perspectives: A View of Family Medicine in New Jersey. 2016;15(3):18.
An overview of telemedicine's regulatory landscape in New Jersey and its emerging role in primary care, written for the state academy's quarterly journal.
Read the article
By Jessica Runyon

Introduction

According to the American Telemedicine Association (ATA), more than 15 million Americans received remote medical care in 2015. While there are significant and reasonable concerns about the implementation and utilization of telemedicine, there are also potential benefits and, of course, hurdles to be overcome, practice standards and licensure portability being the biggest.

Telemedicine in New Jersey

While New Jersey does not specifically authorize telemedicine in its statutes, existing physician licensing laws when applied to telemedicine require a physician be licensed in New Jersey prior to providing services to a patient in the state (N.J.S.A. § 45:9-6.). There are two minor exceptions to this rule (N.J.S.A. § 45:9-21). Subsection (c) of N.J.S.A. § 45:9-21 states a physician or surgeon of another US state who is authorized to practice medicine in that state is not required to obtain a New Jersey license to practice telemedicine, providing the practitioner does not open a practice in the state. N.J.A.C. §§ 13:35-7.1A and -7.5 also indirectly impact telemedicine by requiring all patients in the state to receive a physical exam before medication can be prescribed or dispensed — a regulation that would limit telemedicine services, at least to the extent that drug therapy is included.

Legislation to define telemedicine services was under consideration by the New Jersey Legislature (S291/A1464), which defined "telemedicine" as the delivery of a health care service using electronic communications, information technology, or other electronic or technological means to bridge the gap between a health care provider at a distant site and a patient at an originating site — excluding, in isolation, audio-only phone conversation, email, instant messaging, text, or fax.

Broader Perspectives

The American Academy of Family Physicians (AAFP) defines telemedicine as the use of medical information exchanged from one site to another through electronic communications, encompassing varying processes and services intended to enrich the delivery of medical care and improve patient health status. According to the AAFP, this can include subspecialist consults conducted as live remote visits or the transmission of video or diagnostic images for later review, as well as the remote monitoring and transmission of patient vital signs to a physician's office.

Telemedicine in Primary Care

Telemedicine can complement primary care practice by allowing the office to offer expanded services for non-emergency patients who need advice but not necessarily an office visit. The integration of telemedicine services into primary care has some promising benefits, though more studies are needed to determine its long-term impact. One thing is known: while telemedicine can be effective as face-to-face care, it will never replace the face-to-face visit.

A explainer on Delaware's State Innovation Model practice transformation project, written for primary care practices considering enrollment.

By Jessica Runyon, Program Manager, New Jersey Academy of Family Physicians

The Delaware State Innovations Model (SIM) Practice Transformation Services project is well underway. The intent of this project is to raise the bar on health, and healthcare, throughout Delaware via the process of practice transformation. The main goals for this project (referred to as the Quadruple Aim) comprise better health, improved healthcare quality and patient experience, lower healthcare costs to all of Delaware's residents, and improved professional satisfaction levels for primary care physicians and other healthcare providers throughout the state.

What is practice transformation?

Practice transformation can be defined in several ways; generally, the goals of transformation comprise developing a team-based care structure, a set of quality improvement standards to strive for, and a focus on patient engagement and satisfaction. For this project, DCHI defines transformation as "a high-level change in clinical and operational process to help you care for your entire panel of patients." The steps involved often focus on care management resources targeted to the highest-risk patients, transitional care planning, improving access to care, preventive care outreach, and the integration of behavioral health into the primary care setting.

Practices that enroll to receive free transformation services will achieve significant improvements in patient access to care and satisfaction levels; enhanced team-based care strategies; advances in population health and care management and planning; increased use of self-care for chronic condition management; and developments in performance measurement and quality improvement initiatives.

What services are provided through this grant-funded project?

The New Jersey Academy of Family Physicians (NJAFP), in collaboration with the Delaware Academy of Family Physicians (DAFP), was awarded the role as vendor for Delaware's Practice Transformation Services Project. Our services combine robust, accredited, evidence-based educational content with quality improvement science to guide primary care practices along the journey to population-level, value-based care, using expert faculty, validated QI survey tools, data review, and on-site interviews and observations to achieve each practice's improvement goals.

Avoiding the Rabbit Hole, or How Not to Get Lost in Your EHR Full text
Runyon J. Perspectives: A View of Family Medicine in New Jersey. 2014;13(4):22.
A look at EHR documentation standards and patient-safety risk, prompted by a high-profile Ebola case involving a documentation breakdown.
Read the article
By Jessica Runyon, Project Facilitator, New Jersey Academy of Family Physicians

We have all heard by now that the electronic health record (EHR) system by Texas Health Resources (THR) was not at fault when Texas Health Presbyterian Hospital in Dallas sent Thomas Eric Duncan home after he admitted to visiting Liberia, complained of symptoms that could be caused by the Ebola virus, and admitted to having been in contact with infected individuals. Mr. Duncan died from the virus ten days after his first visit to the Dallas emergency department.

After the hospital initially issued a statement saying that "a flaw in the interaction between the physician and nursing portion of the EHR caused the patient's travel history to not appear in the physician's standard workflow," THR issued a correction two days later stating that the EHR was not to blame, but hasn't elaborated any further.

Despite the lack of EHR culpability in this unfortunate situation, the controversy reopens a necessary dialogue about the importance of setting up an EHR system carefully, and the necessity of regular reviews and maintenance. This highlights a very important piece of the documentation process — and one that NJAFP stresses to the practices it works with: the need to establish, in advance, a set of standard operating procedures (SOPs) for EHR documentation that are easily accessible as a resource for all staff, and are included in all EHR-related training.

EHR systems can provide significant flexibility for a practice, and while this allows practices to model these systems according to their needs, it also creates a conundrum — sometimes, too many options can lead to chaos. Hardeep Singh, a researcher at the Michael E. DeBakey VA Medical Center's Center for Innovations in Quality, Effectiveness and Safety, put it plainly: "People are mostly working to meet the federal requirements and not paying as much attention to the patient safety issues."

In response to these concerns, Singh and colleagues developed the Safety Assurance Factors for EHR Resilience (SAFER) Guides, designed to help healthcare organizations conduct self-assessments to optimize the safety and safe use of EHRs across a variety of areas, including a checklist of recommended practices, downloadable reference materials, and follow-up tools.

When working with practices and clients, NJAFP has always strongly recommended the development and adoption of guidelines and SOPs to ensure regular monitoring of EHR documentation by care team members, with monitoring performed at a regularly defined interval and results reported back to leadership.

Adolescent Influenza Immunization Full text
Runyon J, Barrett TJ. Your Academy in Action. Grant-funded by NJ Department of Health's Vaccine Preventable Disease Program. October 2015.
A herd-immunity explainer and practical toolkit for family physicians working to raise adolescent flu vaccination rates.
Read the article
By Jessica Runyon and Theresa J. Barrett, PhD

Flu season is the perfect time to review the importance of complying with the recommended vaccination schedules for adolescents. While the rate for flu-related hospitalizations in the 5-17 year old population varies each year, immunization for adolescents remains the best way to keep these numbers down. In addition to infection and hospitalization rates for adolescents, it's important to consider the impact that adolescent vaccinations have on the community as a whole.

To understand why, it helps to understand "herd effect" and "herd immunity threshold" as they relate to immunization and indirect protection from infection. Transmission of a communicable disease follows a predictable pattern when introduced into a fully susceptible population — one case leading to four, then sixteen, and so on. However, if 75% of a population is immune, each case leads to only one further transmission, a progression that can be sustained over time provided that proportion remains immune. In order to reach the herd immunity threshold for influenza in the United States, 80% of healthy individuals and 90% of the high-risk population would need to be vaccinated — yet the current U.S. vaccination rate for influenza is only 47.1%.

Vaccination is not only a valuable means of protecting the individual; it also offers indirect protection for those who may be susceptible to disease but cannot be vaccinated, as well as those at risk for developing complications from infection. Family physicians are crucial to achieving the herd immunity threshold, but increasing adolescent vaccination rates poses a real challenge. Practical steps include offering walk-in vaccination-only hours, leveraging patient portals and social media for reminders, coordinating with local schools and parent groups, and hosting flu clinics at schools with parental consent.

Lessons From the Collaborative Full text
Runyon J. Perspectives: A View of Family Medicine in New Jersey. 2017;16(3):15. (CME)
Practical communication techniques for primary care teams working with patients who have major depressive disorder.
Read the article
By Jessica Runyon, Program Manager, Healthcare Transformation and Quality Improvement Team for NJAFP

The Family Medicine Residency Program & Community Health Center Learning Collaborative has been coming together for over seven years to work on quality improvement projects. The Collaborative is currently working to improve the quality of care for patients with Major Depressive Disorder. One topic from the Collaborative's last learning session was interacting with depressed patients.

Between 20–22% of patients with depression receive treatment in the primary care setting, so it's important that the healthcare team knows how to interact with this population. It can be difficult or uncomfortable to interact with a patient suffering from depression, especially when a patient expresses suicidal ideation or begins to share excessive information during an intake interview.

One method for mitigating difficult situations is adapting a guiding style of communication — a style between "directing" (providing information, advice, or instruction) and "following" (listening, seeking to understand, and refraining from offering personal opinion). This guiding style employs accurate empathy, using either simple reflection (paraphrasing back what the patient said) or complex reflection (adding substantial meaning or emphasis) to convey a deeper picture. Other methods include sitting with silence and using non-threatening words or phrases such as "stressed" or "overwhelmed" instead of "depressed," avoiding false reassurances, and not taking it personally if a patient lies or becomes irritable.

When dealing with patients who are depressed, it's important to always ask about suicide directly — 45% of individuals who died by suicide visited their primary care physician within one month of death. Not asking about suicide reinforces the stigma associated with it. Patients with depression value how the healthcare team interacts with them: showing acceptance and interest, listening carefully, communicating clearly, collaborating on manageable goals, and advocating for greater patient self-compassion and self-care.

This is a sensitive topic. If you or someone you know is struggling, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) is available 24/7.

Perspectives2017
Beyond the Blues: Major Depressive Disorder Learning Session Recap Full text
Runyon J. Perspectives: A View of Family Medicine in New Jersey. 2017;16(3):27-8.
A recap of NJAFP's annual Learning Collaborative session on major depressive disorder, including a Healthcare Theatre simulation on communicating with patients in crisis.
Read the article
By Jessica Runyon, Program Manager, Healthcare Transformation and Quality Improvement Team for NJAFP

One of NJAFP's quality improvement & education (QIE) projects is the Family Medicine Residency Program & Community Health Center Learning Collaborative. Every year, the Collaborative focuses on a different clinical indication — this year's indication was major depressive disorder (MDD). The Collaborative has been operating for seven years and currently comprises nineteen practices across Pennsylvania and New Jersey, submitting data and participating in educational activities aimed at improving how they screen for and treat MDD.

Sixty-five people attended the kickoff Learning Session, including physicians, behavioral health providers, nurses, medical assistants, and front-desk and billing staff. Presentations covered the Chronic Care Model as applied to depression treatment, proper empanelment of patients with MDD, workflows for visits, and a Plan-Do-Study-Act activity aimed at managing the MDD patient registry, alongside clinical breakouts on pathophysiology, treatment options, and managing side effects.

Highlights

One of the highlights was a pair of back-to-back non-clinical breakout sessions on communicating with depressed patients and patients who might be in crisis — a workshop led by a clinical psychology doctoral student trained in CBT, followed by an unscripted simulation performed by the University of Delaware's Healthcare Theatre. Rather than using scripts, theatre students are trained to portray patients and family members so that learners can practice technical and interpersonal skills in a safe environment, using a "freeze frame" process where the facilitator pauses the scene to ask the audience for feedback before having a learner step in.

During one simulation, an actor portrayed a very distraught patient. The learner checking her in — front desk staff — suggested the patient needed someone to talk to right away rather than wait in the busy public waiting room, and wanted to call a medical assistant or nurse to sit with her. Collaborative faculty member Dr. William Warning responded that "each staff person in every practice should feel empowered enough to be clinical on some level, and we should have a culture in our practices that doesn't penalize staff for being patient-focused, even if that means their work gets delayed."

Learners responded very positively to the session overall, with some attendees telling NJAFP staff it was the best Learning Session in the seven years they'd participated.

Probiotics: A Promising Treatment for 700 Million People Worldwide? Full text
Runyon J. Stellar Blue Communications. November 28, 2023. Originally published on Stellar Blue Communications; no longer available online.
A research-backed look at the gut-brain axis and emerging evidence on probiotics for PTSD, depression, and anxiety.
Read the article
By Jay Runyon · Nov 28, 2023 · 3 min read

Recently, I was alerted to a very interesting study by Dr. Shanlin Ke and team from the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health. The study, published in Nature Mental Health, looked into the possible effect a Mediterranean diet can have on the severity of symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in women.

The Gut-Brain Axis

In order to better understand how this is possible, we must look into how what we eat can affect how well we feel. The gut-brain axis, which has been talked about for quite some time, provides a communication pathway between emotion and cognition within the brain and gastrointestinal functions. More recently, research has begun to focus on the microbiota-gut-brain axis — taking into account the microorganisms that live within and on our bodies as contributing factors in regulating gut-brain function, a modulation that can be influenced by internal and external factors like diet, stress, and environmental stressors.

The Impact

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 3.6% of the worldwide population has PTSD, which affects people who have experienced or witnessed one or more traumatic or terrifying events. The severity of symptoms varies and isn't directly related to the level of violence in these events, but can be debilitating. In 2019, the WHO estimated that approximately 970 million people worldwide were dealing with some type of mental disorder — a number that worsened significantly in 2020, coinciding with the pandemic. Approximately 717 million of them are dealing specifically with depression, anxiety, and/or PTSD.

Mediterranean vs. Other Diets

Dr. Ke and fellow researchers focused on the diet, stool sample findings, and reported symptoms of 191 women who were participating in a separate longitudinal study. The researchers found evidence demonstrating that the severity of PTSD symptoms was lessened in the cohort of women who followed a Mediterranean diet, compared to those who consumed more red and processed meats. The former had higher levels of a bacteria (Eubacterium eligens) linked to many components of a Mediterranean diet, found in much lower levels in the rest of the study population.

"In a future study, we will attempt to validate the efficacy of probiotics as a method to prevent PTSD."

Next Steps

Examples of the microbiota-gut-brain axis communication can be seen in something called dysbiosis — an imbalance of the intestinal microbiota linked to central nervous system disorders, including depression and anxiety. If the research findings in Dr. Ke et al.'s study suggest that probiotics could prevent PTSD, and the science links their findings to symptom improvement in people suffering from PTSD, it warrants further investigation into the possible inclusion of probiotics among the already available evidence-based therapies — not only for PTSD, but for depression and anxiety as well.

This piece discusses PTSD, depression, and anxiety in a research context. If you're personally affected by any of these, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) and SAMHSA's National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) are available 24/7.
